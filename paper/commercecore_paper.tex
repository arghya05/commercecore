\begin{abstract}
Small language models offer a practical way to convert shopping queries into executable constraints, but partial extraction accuracy can obscure whether the complete request is represented correctly. We study CommerceCore, a Qwen3-1.7B model adapted with QLoRA on 2,130 examples spanning entity segmentation, query constraints, and catalog attributes. The recorded run takes 75.2 minutes at an estimated GPU cost of \$0.90. Archived served-model predictions reproduce mean constraint-set F1 of 0.683 on 20 development queries, with seven exact-set matches; six harder diagnostic queries yield F1 of 0.400 and no exact-set matches. A separate aggregate records entity-set F1 of 0.498 on 993 QueryNER examples. We connect these results to the complete training-loss trace, eight checkpoint evaluations, and an executable audit of data and evaluation integrity. The audit identifies eight groups of inconsistent synthetic targets, test examples used for checkpoint selection, and an API baseline prompt covering only two of 17 entity labels. These conditions limit comparative and generalization claims. The contribution is a reproducible empirical account of low-budget adaptation and the gap between partial extraction scores and faithful constraint sets, with public code, model artifacts, and result-level provenance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
Shopping queries express requirements on product attributes, price, and usage. Converting ``black waterproof trainers under 80 pounds'' into a color constraint alone produces valid JSON and earns partial extraction credit, yet loses two requirements. An executable search representation must preserve both the meaning and the completeness of the request. This distinction motivates evaluating whole constraint sets alongside per-query F1.

A small generative model can share one structured-output interface across query segmentation, constraint extraction, and catalog attributes. CommerceCore implements this approach with Qwen3-1.7B~\cite{qwen3,qwenmodel}, adapting it on human entity annotations and synthetic commerce examples. Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline} summarizes the training mixture and evaluation stages. The practical question is how much evidence a low-budget adaptation study provides about extraction quality and deployment readiness.

The archived run demonstrates inexpensive adaptation, while its predictions expose a gap between partial overlap and complete extraction: served-model F1 of 0.683 corresponds to only seven exact matches among 20 development queries. On six harder cases, F1 remains 0.400 despite zero exact matches. Understanding these observations requires examining how targets were generated, which examples informed checkpoint selection, and how baselines were prompted.

We organize the study around three questions. \textbf{RQ1} characterizes recorded compute and task performance. \textbf{RQ2} tests whether the data and evaluation protocols support model comparisons. \textbf{RQ3} examines checkpoint behavior and semantic failures in the saved predictions.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A traceable adaptation study.} We document the three-task mixture, QLoRA configuration, complete 1,200-step loss trace, eight checkpoints, and released serving artifact (Sections~\ref{sec:method} and~\ref{sec:results}).
\item \textbf{Result-level verification.} We reproduce the archived training mixture in order and rescore all 20 served-model and six diagnostic predictions, reporting F1 together with exact-set correctness (Section~\ref{sec:results}).
\item \textbf{An executable integrity audit.} We identify inconsistent targets, checkpoint-selection exposure, and restricted baseline label coverage, and connect each finding to public evidence (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}; Appendix~\ref{app:provenance}).
\end{enumerate}
The contribution is empirical: it establishes an inspectable implementation and its evaluation boundaries. The available study does not isolate gains from fine-tuning, multitask learning, or synthetic data; those questions require the controlled experiments specified in Appendix~\ref{app:future}.

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Commerce query understanding.}
QueryNER provides human-annotated shopping queries with 17 entity types and evaluates supervised BERT and XLM-R taggers, including domain-adapted BERT~\cite{queryner}. These are relevant specialist baselines for a generative segmentation model. CommerceCore serializes label--text pairs as JSON; its per-query set metric is not interchangeable with token-indexed sequence-labeling evaluation. We make no leaderboard comparison against the original QueryNER results.

\paragraph{Small-model specialization.}
Licardo and Tankovic~\cite{licardo2025} study QLoRA adaptation of a one-billion-parameter Llama 3.2 model for multilingual commerce intent recognition and deployment quantization. This is close prior art for the small-model specialization strategy. CommerceCore instead produces constraint and entity sets, includes human-annotated data, and examines label and evaluation integrity. Neither changing the backbone nor using synthetic data establishes methodological novelty.

\paragraph{Multitask adaptation.}
EcomGPT~\cite{ecomgpt} studies commerce instruction tuning using EcomInstruct and a chain-of-task formulation. CommerceCore uses a much smaller mixture with plain task prefixes, without chain-of-task supervision. LoRA learns low-rank updates to frozen weights~\cite{lora}; QLoRA combines such updates with a quantized backbone~\cite{qlora}. We adopt this established recipe. The catalog task is auxiliary and lacks a separate evaluation, so we cannot attribute gains to sharing tasks rather than training specialists.

\section{CommerceCore: Tasks, Data, and Adaptation}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Output contracts and training mixture}
For a query $x$, constraint extraction predicts $C(x)=\{(f_j,o_j,v_j)\}_{j=1}^{m}$, where $f_j$ is a schema field, $o_j$ a comparison operator, and $v_j$ a string-valued canonical value. Synthetic targets cover nine fields: color, material, size, waterproof status, added-sugar presence, nut presence, price in GBP, power source, and warranty duration in months. Boolean values are strings. Historical labels map ``under 80'' to \code{lte}; scoring follows that convention without claiming strict linguistic equivalence.

Entity segmentation predicts $(\ell,s)$ pairs, with QueryNER label $\ell$ and span text $s$. Local records preserve tokens and half-open token offsets; training converts spans to space-joined text. Catalog extraction maps a listing to an attribute dictionary. Thus three tasks are trained, while only two have task-level evaluation artifacts.

The QueryNER builder shuffles 7,841 local training records with seed 42 and selects 1,500 (Table~\ref{tab:data}). The local test file contains 993 records. Both identify source revision \path{f2f9173d14243496dbcf017a9dc2ded74989a45e}. We report counts from these files rather than assuming all published versions have identical counts. The dataset card declares CC BY 4.0~\cite{querynerdata}, and local provenance identifies Amazon ESCI as the source of the underlying queries. An offline audit finds no normalized exact query overlap between the local train and test files; this does not exclude paraphrase overlap or pretraining contamination.

\subsection{Synthetic generation and validation}
The query generator samples a structured scenario in code and asks an API model, identified as \code{claude-sonnet-4-5}, to render it as a shopping query. Query and catalog data cover fashion, grocery, and general merchandise equally. Generated targets constitute weak supervision, not new human annotation.

The query path constructs an intermediate contract by finding literal value substrings or predefined Boolean surface forms, omitting constraints with no match. A validator checks schema structure, span binding, and a blocklist of generic phrases. Although the validator supports simulated execution, the scaled query-generation call supplies neither tool calls nor a catalog; execution correctness is not checked on that path.

The writer then serializes the \emph{original scenario constraints}, rather than the filtered contract that passed validation. Acceptance of that contract therefore does not guarantee grounding of every saved target. Substring binding also cannot prove completeness or semantic fidelity. For example, finding an attribute word does not by itself resolve whether it is negated.

\subsection{Training configuration}
The base is Qwen3-1.7B, distributed under Apache 2.0~\cite{qwenmodel}. Training uses 4-bit NF4 with double quantization and bfloat16 computation; LoRA rank 16, alpha 32, and dropout 0.05; and adapters on the attention query/key/value/output and feed-forward gate/up/down projections. Gradient checkpointing is enabled. For prompt $p_i$ and retained completion tokens $y_i$, each optimizer step averages token-normalized losses over eight accumulated examples $\mathcal{B}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{B}}(\theta)=-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}}\frac{1}{|y_i|}\sum_{t\in y_i}\log p_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid p_i,y_{i,<t}).
\end{equation}
Prompt tokens are masked, an end-of-sequence token is appended, and the concatenated sequence is truncated to 512 tokens. AdamW uses learning rate $10^{-4}$, microbatch one, and eight accumulation steps. The once-shuffled mixture is cycled sequentially without task reweighting or epoch reshuffling. The training seed is 42. There are 1,200 optimizer steps, with evaluation every 150 steps, corresponding to approximately 4.51 passes through 2,130 rows. The selected step 750 represents 2.82 passes. Checkpoint task decoding is greedy with 100 new tokens, plain prefixes, and no constrained JSON decoder.

\section{Experimental Setup and Evidence Audit}
\label{sec:protocol}
\subsection{Metrics and parsing}
Historical scores are means of \emph{per-query exact-set F1}, not label-macro-F1. For predicted set $P_i$ and gold set $G_i$,
\begin{equation}
 F_{1,i}=\frac{2|P_i\cap G_i|}{|P_i|+|G_i|},\qquad
 \Fmacro=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n F_{1,i}.
\end{equation}
Two empty sets score one. Constraints match exact field/operator strings and stringified values. Entity matching uses the exact label and lowercased, stripped span text; token offsets are discarded. Set conversion removes duplicates, so repeated identical predictions are not penalized and identical span text at different positions is indistinguishable.

The parser extracts a JSON list with a regular expression; missing or invalid JSON becomes an empty list. This conflates parse failure with a valid empty prediction. One of the 20 constraint development queries has empty gold, giving an always-empty system mean F1 of 0.050. For the six saved diagnostic predictions, we additionally compute pooled precision, recall, micro-F1, and exact-set accuracy $n^{-1}\sum_i\mathbf{1}[P_i=G_i]$.

\subsection{Data splits and selection exposure}
The 20 author-written constraint queries comprise seven fashion, seven grocery, and six general-merchandise examples. Despite the filename \code{held\_out\_eval\_set.jsonl}, all 20 are evaluated at every checkpoint. We therefore treat them as \emph{development data}. Their normalized query strings do not exactly match the synthetic training file, but they are not an untouched final evaluation.

The training script likewise evaluates the first 30 QueryNER test records at each checkpoint. The later 993-example score includes these records and is selection-exposed. Full-test per-example predictions are absent, preventing rescoring on the remaining 963 records or a paired comparison. Retrospective removal would also not recreate a prospectively frozen evaluation.

Six additional author-written queries provide diagnostics for composition, negation, permissive language, and unit/vocabulary normalization. Their strings do not exactly match the current synthetic file or development set. They remain a small convenience sample with schema-specific, sometimes incomplete gold labels; they are not a representative out-of-distribution benchmark.

\subsection{Baseline comparability}
The QueryNER API script evaluates the \emph{first 50} test examples and requests only \code{core\_product\_type} or \code{modifier}. On that sample, 52 of 119 annotated spans have other labels. Let $k_i$ denote the number of gold label--text pairs allowed by the prompt and $g_i$ the total number of gold pairs. A perfect predictor restricted to the allowed labels obtains
\begin{equation}
 F^{\mathrm{restricted}}_{1,i}=\frac{2k_i}{k_i+g_i}.
\end{equation}
The optimal prediction includes every permitted gold pair and no incorrect pair, since incorrect additions increase only the denominator. Averaging this bound over the 50 examples gives \RestrictedPromptCeiling{}. This is a prompt-compliance ceiling, not a bound on a model that ignores the instruction. Different samples and label vocabularies preclude interpreting the available scores as evidence of CommerceCore's superiority.

The constraint API scripts use two demonstrations but do not specify the full field vocabulary. Historical IDs are \code{claude-haiku-4-5-20251001}, \code{claude-sonnet-5}, \code{gpt-4o-mini}, and \code{gpt-5}; these are recorded identifiers, not claims about current availability or provider tiers. Their numerical aggregates are preserved in Appendix~\ref{app:baselines}. Per-request metadata and raw generations are incomplete.

The open-model script uses Qwen3.5-2B and SmolLM3-3B with plain text prompts and 150 new tokens, without model-specific chat templates or explicit reasoning-mode settings. Both have empty parsed predictions on all six diagnostic queries. Without raw generations, empty answers cannot be distinguished from truncation or parser failure. These comparisons do not isolate fine-tuning or scale.

\subsection{Uncertainty and provenance}
The full QueryNER file records mean F1 0.498474 and a 10,000-resample percentile interval [0.474948, 0.522320]. We retain this as a \emph{recorded interval}, since its score vector is absent. It describes query resampling under the implemented metric, not training-seed variation, selection bias, or prompt uncertainty. The repeated constraint-baseline script instead bootstraps 60 entries from three calls on 20 queries. Those entries are not independent queries; valid query-level inference would cluster repeats and preferably estimate paired differences. We therefore do not use its pooled intervals for comparative inference or infer population uncertainty from six deliberately chosen examples.

\subsection{Checkpoint selection and behavioral drift}
At each checkpoint, the model answers five fixed prompts about a capital city, weather, arithmetic, a primary color, and a sentence completion. Let $A$ and $B$ be sets of lowercased whitespace-delimited output tokens from the checkpoint and frozen base. The diagnostic averages
\begin{equation}
S(A,B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{\max(|A|,|B|)},
\end{equation}
with one for two empty sets and zero if only one is empty. It measures lexical agreement: a correct paraphrase can lower it, while a shared wrong answer can raise it. It is not a validated measure of retained general capability.

\input{figures/training_dynamics.tex}

The historical selection note chooses step 750, where output similarity is highest (0.6143), constraint F1 is 0.7250, and QueryNER subset F1 is 0.5562. At step 1,200 these are 0.4886, 0.7400, and 0.5298. Step 600 maximizes QueryNER subset F1 and step 1,050 maximizes constraint F1 (Figure~\ref{fig:checkpoints}). Pareto analysis over all three logged signals retains steps 150, 300, 600, 750, 900, and 1,050. Step 750 is one nondominated choice, not a uniquely optimal checkpoint. It was selected after observing the trajectories, without a prespecified scalar objective or threshold. The later similarity decline establishes output drift on these probes, not catastrophic forgetting or a causal effect of overtraining.

The complete 1,200-step loss trace, approximate epoch summaries, and exact losses at all eight checkpoints are included in Appendix~\ref{app:training-logs}. The mean logged loss decreases from 0.2136 over steps 1--266 to 0.0246 over the final partial pass (steps 1,066--1,200). These are training-loss summaries, not held-out loss estimates; the decline does not establish better extraction accuracy or preserved general capability.

\section{Efficiency and Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
\paragraph{Recorded compute and cost.}
The run summary records 4,510.6 seconds (75.18 minutes); at the encoded historical rate of \$0.72 per GPU hour, the estimated charge is \$0.9021. The timer includes periodic evaluation and checkpointing but excludes earlier loading and base-probe generation. Peak \code{torch.cuda.max\_memory\_allocated} is recorded as 2.13 GiB, which excludes reserved and non-PyTorch device memory. It is not a minimum hardware requirement. The project identifies an RTX PRO 4500 with 32 GB; the summary does not embed a hardware trace.

The cumulative generation ledger records 1,630 calls and an estimated \$\LedgerCost{} at its encoded token prices. This is generation history, not a complete project budget. Evaluation APIs, startup, idle time, storage, and engineering effort are not fully reconciled. No sustained-load serving trace is available for independently auditing latency percentiles or throughput. For hourly rate $r$, sustained throughput $q$, and utilization $u$, compute-only cost per query is $r/(u q)$. Batching capacity and utilization cannot be inferred from a few request timings; no measured superiority in serving economics is claimed.

\paragraph{Causal and comparative limitations.}
The run supports implementation feasibility (RQ1). It lacks a matched unfine-tuned Qwen3-1.7B evaluation, supervised encoder baseline, deterministic baseline, and controlled single-task or data-source ablations. The existing comparisons do not establish superiority (RQ2), and the three synthetic verticals are not independent merchant populations. Only one training seed is available. Neither the contribution of catalog supervision nor positive task transfer is measured.

\paragraph{Supervision and robustness.}
The writer/validator discrepancy and conflicting targets show that scenario-first generation alone does not ensure faithful labels. Incomplete predictions are consistent with incomplete supervision, but a causal test requires retraining on repaired data under a matched protocol. The catalog targets also need their own fidelity audit. Five output-similarity probes and six diagnostic queries identify behaviors (RQ3), not general-capability retention or population robustness. A confirmatory design is specified in Appendix~\ref{app:future}; its experiments are not reported as completed.

\paragraph{Practical use and broader impact.}
The service is a research interface. Valid JSON can contain unsupported or conflicting constraints, and empty lists can mask parsing failures. Execution should distinguish those states and preserve user intent, particularly for dietary or allergen requirements that these results cannot certify. English queries and narrow synthetic schemas limit representativeness. Self-hosting can provide architectural control over data flow, but no privacy guarantee or security audit is established here. Dataset attribution and underlying query provenance should accompany redistribution; synthetic labels should remain distinguishable from human annotation.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}
The public artifacts are the \href{https://github.com/arghya05/commercecore}{GitHub repository}, \href{https://huggingface.co/arghya2030/commercecore-qwen3-1.7b}{Hugging Face model card}, and \href{https://huggingface.co/arghya2030/commercecore-qwen3-1.7b/tree/425c4a52e6a0e73fd6770ee0918851a132821d3d}{versioned model files and archive}. The latter includes merged weights, tokenizer files, checkpoint adapters, training data, logs, and evaluation records. Availability does not by itself verify that the released weights reproduce every recorded run. Appendix~\ref{app:provenance} provides clickable evidence links, fixed repository revisions, and instructions for checking the numbers.

The standard-library script \href{https://github.com/arghya05/commercecore/blob/main/paper/audit_evidence.py}{\nolinkurl{paper/audit_evidence.py}} recounts rows, reproduces the archived seed-42 mixture in order, checks exact overlaps and conflicting target sets, computes the restricted-label ceiling, rescores the 20 served-model and six diagnostic predictions, and regenerates loss and checkpoint data. It saves \href{https://github.com/arghya05/commercecore/blob/main/paper/evidence/audit_results.json}{machine-readable results}, reconstructed sample IDs, and SHA-256 hashes. Small archive inputs and the historical mixture are mirrored under \path{paper/evidence/archive/} so the audit can run from the GitHub checkout without downloading model weights. Full-test per-example QueryNER generations and a complete pinned training environment remain unavailable, and several original inference scripts retain pod-specific paths. These offline checks are distinct from rerunning model inference.

\section{Conclusion}
CommerceCore documents low-budget adaptation for structured commerce queries through public training and inference artifacts. Its archived results show why partial set overlap is insufficient to characterize faithful extraction: mean F1 of 0.683 on 20 development queries corresponds to seven complete sets, while six harder cases yield no complete set despite mean F1 of 0.400. The training history and integrity audit explain how to interpret this evidence without attributing unmeasured causes. Consistent target construction, matched baselines, and an independent final test are the next requirements for establishing whether the approach generalizes. The released code, predictions, logs, and audit make that extension directly inspectable.
